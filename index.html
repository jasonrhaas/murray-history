D 351st Bombardment Squadron, 100th Bomb Group (Heavy), Eighth Air Force

MurrayLirette

Houma, Louisiana  ·  Bombardier, B-17G Katie

A bombardier in the nose of a B-17 called Katie. On 11 May 1944 the aeroplane came apart around him over Belgium. Seven of the ten men aboard were killed. This is the record of how he came to be there, what happened that afternoon, and what followed.

This is a working record of Murray Lirette's war, assembled from the 100th Bomb Group Foundation's personnel and mission databases, the group's own mission narrative for 11 May 1944, surviving crew statements, captured German reports, three wartime clippings from his home-town paper, and recent Louisiana news coverage. It marks what is documented separately from what is merely likely, because on several points the record is thinner than the story deserves.

Three marks run through the text, because the evidence behind each claim varies a great deal in quality:

Documented  Supported by a surviving record — a mission narrative, a personnel file, a crew statement, a captured German report, or a contemporary newspaper.

Probable  A strong inference from matching details, not yet confirmed against a primary document.

Unconfirmed  Reported somewhere, but not yet corroborated.

DPart oneHouma to the bombsight

He was a Terrebonne Parish boy. The 100th Bomb Group Foundation's file gives his home town at the time of service simply as Houma, Louisiana, and his serial number as O-767531 — the O prefix marking him as a commissioned officer rather than an enlisted man. Documented

On 16 October 1943 the Houma Times ran a notice that Murray Lirette had been awarded his wings. That dates the end of his training and the start of the short window between qualification and combat. Bombardier school in that period ran roughly twelve to eighteen weeks and ended with a commission as second lieutenant and the silver bombardier's badge. Documented

The crew he did not fly with

There is a photograph in the Foundation's file of his original crew, taken during stateside training at Pyote, Texas — the vast, wind-scoured B-17 base in the West Texas desert that its own airmen called the Rattlesnake Bomber Base. Lirette is kneeling at bottom right.

The caption, again from Lirette, records what happened to that crew: the pilot and co-pilot were pulled off and sent to a B-29 training unit. With the crew broken up, Lirette was reassigned, and became bombardier on the Jack Hunter crew shortly before it left for England. Documented

A posting decision made for administrative convenience put him in a different aeroplane on a different day. It very probably saved his life, and it certainly cost him seven friends.

What a bombardier actually did

The job is worth setting out, because it is often misunderstood. In a B-17G the bombardier sat in the extreme nose, ahead of everyone, inside a frame of perspex with almost nothing solid around him. For most of the flight he was a gunner, working the chin turret and the nose guns.

On the bomb run itself he took over. The Norden bombsight was coupled through the autopilot to the aircraft's controls, so from the initial point to bombs away the bombardier was effectively flying the aeroplane — holding it straight, level and predictable through the most heavily defended few minutes of the mission, at exactly the moment when every gun below had a stable firing solution. Everyone on board understood that those minutes were the price of hitting anything at all.

DPart twoThorpe Abbotts, April 1944

The Hunter crew joined the 100th Bomb Group on 5 April 1944 and was assigned to the 351st Bomb Squadron at Thorpe Abbotts, a bomber station scattered across farmland near Diss in Norfolk. Documented

They had arrived at a group with a reputation. The 100th had flown its first mission in June 1943 and had since acquired the name the Bloody Hundredth — nine aircraft lost over Regensburg in August 1943, twelve over Münster that October, fifteen over Berlin on a single day in March 1944, five weeks before Lirette reported in.

The reputation deserves one honest qualification, which the group's own veterans made themselves. Across 306 missions the 100th lost 177 aircraft — fewer than the 91st Bomb Group's 197. What marked the Hundredth was not the total but the shape of it: long stretches of ordinary attrition broken by single days of catastrophe, with roughly half its entire loss concentrated into about eight missions. Navigator Harry Crosby put it in his memoir as a matter of losing big rather than losing often. Documented

The crew's operational life

It was short. The top turret gunner, Arthur Wellingham, kept a journal of the crew's missions running from 12 April to 8 May 1944, which brackets their operational career almost exactly: roughly four weeks of flying. He was killed on the next mission after his last entry.

Three sorties can be attached to the crew by name from the group's records, all of them in Katie:

DateGroup missionTarget

On the 8 May mission the right waist gunner, Samuel Martiello, was wounded — in the backside, as Wellingham recorded it with the flatness of a man who had decided to find things funny. Martiello was off flying status three days later, and so was the ball turret gunner, Ernest Medhurst. Two replacements were put aboard Katie in their places. Documented

The full mission count is not established in the public record. The crew almost certainly flew more than these three between 12 April and 11 May; the group's narrative names individual crews only when something happens to them. Unconfirmed

DPart threeLiège, 11 May 1944

Thursday 11 May 1944 B-17G 42-39983 Katie · 100th Bomb Group mission 115 · Lead Squadron

The group was alerted at half past ten that morning that something would go off in the afternoon. Thirty-six crews sat down to the briefing at half past twelve, where the intelligence officer, Captain Paul Mackesey, gave them the target: the marshalling yards at Liège, in occupied Belgium.

The briefing made the case in numbers. More than fifteen hundred goods wagons moved through those yards every twenty-four hours; the sidings ran to sixteen tracks and could hold two thousand wagons. It was, in short, a hinge in the German rail network feeding France and the Low Countries, four weeks before the Normandy landings. Aachen was the secondary. Documented

Mackesey also told them what to avoid — the heavy batteries around Antwerp and Woensdrecht, the guns at Herenthals off to the right at maximum range — and what was waiting: sixteen heavy guns defending the target itself.

The 100th was leading. Lieutenant Colonel Ollen Turner flew at the head of the 3rd Air Division and of the 13A formation, and Hunter's crew was in the Lead Squadron. Documented

What happened to the aeroplane

The shell went into the wing beside the inboard port engine. Part of the wing came off. The aircraft caught fire and fell into a flaming spin — a spin tight enough that the men inside were pinned by centrifugal force and physically could not move toward the hatches.

Then the fuel tanks went up. In the navigator Richard Heh's own statement, the explosion blew the bombardier and myself out of the plane. The bombardier was Murray Lirette. Documented

Heh had no memory of anything after that until he came to in mid-air, falling, and estimated he had already dropped something like ten thousand feet before he regained consciousness. He got his parachute open. So did Lirette.

The tail gunner, Herbert South, got out of the aircraft under his own power despite a compound fracture of the leg — one of the more remarkable small facts in the file.

A crew flying behind saw the aircraft burning and then simply gone. Another observer reported that it hit near the marshalling yards and exploded, and that two parachutes were seen. Three men in fact survived. Documented

A note on the record

The group's own mission narrative introduces Heh's statement as coming from the co-pilot. He was the navigator; the co-pilot, George Shoesmith, was killed. The Foundation's crew list and Heh's own personnel file both have him as navigator, and his statement describes being in the nose with the bombardier. The narrative appears to have slipped. Probable

Seven killed. Three taken prisoner. One aircraft lost by the group that day, and this was it.

The two substitutes are the hardest part of the file. Anglin and Rupnick were not Katie's men; they were filling in for a wounded gunner and one who was not flying. Both were killed. Rupnick had already outlived one crew — he had come to the 100th in June 1943 with a different one entirely.

What the survivors said afterward

At interrogation that evening, crew after crew complained about the same thing: the bombing altitude had been too low for the number of guns defending the target. They were still giving their accounts when the alert came in for the next day's mission.

DPart fourPrisoner

The German paperwork picks him up almost at once. A captured report records that Lt Murray Lirette was committed to hospital on 13 May 1944, two days after the crash — consistent with injuries taken either in the explosion that threw him clear or on landing. The same report notes that the dead were buried at the Luftwaffe cemetery at the air base the Germans called Trond. Documented

That is almost certainly Sint-Truiden — St. Trond — the Luftwaffe night-fighter station about thirty kilometres west of Liège, which had its own service cemetery. Probable

From hospital he went into the prisoner-of-war system, and as a captured American airman that meant a camp run by the Luftwaffe rather than the army. He was sent to Stalag Luft III at Sagan, in Lower Silesia, about a hundred miles south-east of Berlin — today Żagań, in Poland. Documented

He arrived at a bad moment in that camp's history. Seven weeks before he was shot down, seventy-six men had tunnelled out of Stalag Luft III in the breakout that became known as the Great Escape. Fifty of the recaptured escapers had been murdered on Hitler's direct order, and the news of those killings was being confirmed to the outside world in the same weeks Lirette was making his way there from a German hospital bed.

On 26 August 1944 the Houma Times printed the thing his family had been waiting on: Murray Lirette, prisoner of war, had written home. Documented

The march

On the night of 27 January 1945, with the Red Army closing on Silesia, the Germans emptied Stalag Luft III at a few hours' notice and marched its prisoners west on foot. It was one of the coldest European winters on record. Men walked in shoes that had not been made for it, hauling what they could on improvised sledges, sleeping in barns and factory sheds, and a good number did not finish.

Lirette was on that march and came through it. Documented

His injuries put him back into a German hospital before the end, and he was liberated in 1945 by elements of Patton's Third Army. The precise camp or hospital at liberation is not established in the sources available here. Unconfirmed

On 6 October 1945 the Houma Times reported that he was to be discharged. Documented

DPart fiveThe long part afterwards

He came home to Terrebonne Parish and, on 26 November 1948, married Beverly Belanger of Houma. They raised four daughters, and moved to Slidell, on the north shore of Lake Pontchartrain, which stayed home for the next fifty-nine years. Beverly died on 9 April 2020, after seventy-one years of marriage. Documented

That is the part the war record cannot capture and the part that mattered most: three quarters of a century of ordinary Louisiana life, obtained at Liège on credit that six of his crewmates never got to draw on.

Seven men died in that aeroplane in about four seconds. Three came out of it.

Still turning up

He has stayed close to the group. In May 2025 he attended the 100th Bomb Group reunion at the National WWII Museum in New Orleans, aged 101, alongside two other veterans of the Hundredth — the pilot John "Lucky" Luckadoo, then 103, and the navigator James Rasmussen, also 101 — with more than 360 family members and friends. Luckadoo died that September. Documented

He has also given his own photographs to the Foundation's archive. The portrait and the Pyote crew shot reproduced above are both credited to him, which is why anything is known about the crew he did not fly with.

Belgium, 2026

On 16 May 2026, in Baton Rouge, at 102 years old, Murray Lirette was made a Chevalier de l'Ordre de Léopold — Knight of the Order of Leopold, Belgium's oldest and highest order — presented by the Belgian deputy ambassador and the Honorary Consul for Belgium in Louisiana, Roland Vandenweghe. Documented

The symmetry is hard to miss. He was decorated by the country he was shot down over, eighty-two years after he fell into it.

The same coverage identifies him as the last surviving prisoner of war of the 100th Bomb Group, and as one of only three survivors from his crew. Documented

  • Sint-TruidenThe Luftwaffe airfield west of Liège whose cemetery, according to the German report, received his crew's dead.
  • SaganStalag Luft III, in Silesia. Now Żagań, Poland.
  • West, on footJanuary and February 1945, out of Sagan into one of the century's worst winters.
  • Slidell, LouisianaFifty-nine years of it, with Beverly and four daughters. Where the rest of the life happened.

DPart sevenWhat to look for next

Quite a lot of this record is recoverable, and some of it from people who are still reachable. In rough order of value:

1. Richard Heh's prisoner-of-war journal

This is the single most promising lead on the page, and it is already online. Heh — the navigator who was blown out of the nose beside Lirette — kept a journal in captivity, and his family placed a scan of it with the 100th Bomb Group Foundation in two parts. Two men went out of the same hole in the same aeroplane at the same instant. The odds that Lirette appears in it are very high.

The files are at 100thbg.com/images/Diaries/, as Richard Heh POW Journal Part 1 and Part 2, linked from Heh's personnel page.

2. MACR 4866, in full

The Missing Air Crew Report for Katie is held by the U.S. National Archives and is digitised on the usual subscription services. The Foundation's file quotes from it; the complete document adds the loading list, the eyewitness statements as filed, and the enclosed German capture documents.

3. His own service and prisoner records

The wartime prisoner-of-war data file held by the National Archives in Record Group 389 should give his camp designation and his date and place of liberation, which would close the one substantial gap in part four. A next-of-kin request to the National Personnel Records Center is the route to his individual file — with the standard caveat that the 1973 fire at that building destroyed a very large share of Army personnel records for this period.

4. Murray's own account

The best source on this page is not in an archive. Anything marked Unconfirmed above — the mission list, the camp compound, the route of the march, the hospital at the end — sits in Murray's own memory, and in whatever papers, letters, dog tags and photographs the family still holds. Getting it recorded properly is worth more than any file in Maryland. The 100th Bomb Group Foundation runs an oral-history programme and publishes Splasher Six; the National WWII Museum in New Orleans, thirty miles from Slidell, holds one of the largest oral-history collections in the country and will travel to record.

5. The Wellingham journal

Arthur Wellingham's mission journal, covering 12 April to 8 May 1944, was in family hands as recently as the 1980s, when a surviving crew member wrote about it. It would give the crew's exact mission list — and therefore Lirette's.

6. Belgium and the seven

Local aviation historians around Liège and Sint-Truiden document individual crash sites in considerable detail, and Individual Deceased Personnel Files exist for each of the seven men killed. Between them they should fix where Katie came down and where her dead went afterward.

Open questions, in priority order

  • His exact date of birth, and his parents' names.
  • The complete list of missions he flew between 12 April and 11 May 1944.
  • His prisoner-of-war number, compound, and the date he reached Sagan.
  • His route on the January 1945 march, and which hospital and which unit liberated him.
  • Any American decorations — Purple Heart, Air Medal, Prisoner of War Medal — which the sources here do not list but which his record would very likely support.
  • Where Katie came down, and where the seven are buried today.

DIn shortWhat the record shows

A young man from the Louisiana bayou country qualified as a bombardier in the autumn of 1943, lost his own crew to a reshuffle, and joined another one on its way to England. He flew for about four weeks with the most notorious bomb group in the Eighth Air Force, twice to Berlin. On his last mission he was in the nose of the lead squadron's ship over Liège, put his bombs into the marshalling yards, and seconds later a shell took the wing off the aeroplane.

It fell burning and spinning so hard nobody could move. Then the fuel tanks exploded and threw him out of it. He came to under a parachute, injured, over occupied Belgium, and spent the next year in a German hospital, in Stalag Luft III, on a winter march across Silesia, and in another German hospital before American tanks reached him.

Seven men he had flown with did not get out. He came home, married, raised four daughters, and lived a long and unremarkable life in Slidell — which, after Liège, is the best thing anyone could say about it.

In May 2026, in Baton Rouge, Belgium made him a knight of the Order of Leopold — decorated at last by the country he fell into.
